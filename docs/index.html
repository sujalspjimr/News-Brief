Spanish Voters Go to Polls in High-Stakes General Election

Spain's main party leaders have made their final pitches to voters ahead of Sunday's general election, which could mark a significant political shift for the country. Nearly 39 million eligible voters are casting ballots in an election where opinion polls have pointed to a close finish and a potential change in government, putting an end to Socialist Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez's eight years of leadership. Sánchez has urged progressive voters to turn out and prevent the country from moving backward, warning of a potential right-wing coalition that could reverse social progress. Meanwhile, conservative People's Party (PP) leader Alberto Núñez Feijóo has campaigned on promises of good governance, decency, and truth, dismissing the incumbent's warnings as a campaign of fear.

Key issues dominating the run-in include the cost of living, housing and rental prices, immigration, national debt, and corruption. The conservative PP has held a narrow lead in the polls, though forming a government could require the support of the anti-immigration far-right Vox party, led by Santiago Abascal. If successful, Vox could enter national government for the first time since the end of the Franco dictatorship in 1975. Alternatively, analysts note there remains a possibility of a hung parliament if neither the right-wing coalition nor the left bloc secures a clear majority.

Internationally, the election is viewed as a key test of Europe's broader political shift toward the right, preceding upcoming elections on the continent. Sánchez has positioned himself as a standard-bearer for Europeans frustrated by the Trump administration, while Feijóo has indicated a desire to maintain good relations with US President Donald Trump.

Canada's Supreme Court Largely Upholds Quebec Ban on Religious Symbols

Canada's Supreme Court has largely upheld Quebec's controversial 2019 secularism law, known as Bill 21, which bars public sector workers such as teachers, police officers, judges, and lawyers from wearing conspicuous religious symbols like hijabs, turbans, kippahs, and crosses while on the job. In a closely watched ruling, the court found that Quebec was permitted to shield the law from most court challenges by using the 'notwithstanding clause,' a constitutional mechanism allowing governments to override certain charter rights.

The landmark decision addressed major constitutional questions surrounding the limits of provincial legislative power and minority rights. While the majority of the justices validated the core of the law, the court ruled that the legislation infringes on democratic rights by prohibiting Quebec's elected officials from wearing face coverings for religious reasons. Additionally, some dissenting justices argued that the notwithstanding clause should not authorize laws incompatible with Canada's fundamental character as a free and democratic society.

Supporters of the ruling, including provincial nationalist figures, hailed the decision as a vital victory for state secularism and Quebec's historical commitment to keeping public institutions neutral. Conversely, civil liberties groups and minority advocates sharply criticized the verdict. They argued that the legislation disproportionately harms Muslim women who wear the hijab and weakens fundamental freedoms, effectively turning the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms into a set of mere suggestions.

Prime Minister Mark Carney stated that his government would study the ruling's implications, emphasizing the importance of respecting the rights and liberties of all citizens, including minorities. The case drew immense national attention, resulting in one of the longest hearings and highest numbers of participants in the Supreme Court's history.

Key facts

  • Canada's Supreme Court largely upheld Quebec's Bill 21, a 2019 law banning public sector workers from wearing religious symbols.
  • The 6-1 ruling backed Quebec's use of the constitutional 'notwithstanding clause' to shield the law from most charter challenges.
  • The court ruled that the law does infringe on democratic rights by preventing elected officials from wearing face coverings for religious reasons.
  • The case involved record-breaking participation, with 51 interveners and four days of hearings.
  • Dissenting justices expressed concern that the law infringed on freedom of religion, expression, and equality.

Prime Minister Modi Vows to Implement Women's Reservation Amid Legislative and Delimitation Updates

Prime Minister Narendra Modi has reaffirmed his administration's commitment to implementing a 33 percent reservation for women in Parliament and state legislative assemblies. Speaking after the Union Cabinet cleared a revised legislative package—including the Constitution (132nd Amendment) Bill, 2026, and the delimitation (Second) Bill, 2026—Modi stated that his government would overcome all obstacles to secure greater political representation for women. He also criticized opposing parties, asserting they would not be able to prevent the reform.

The newly cleared package follows the failure of the previous Constitution (131st Amendment) Bill to secure a two-thirds majority in the Lok Sabha in April. The updated proposals include a uniform 50 percent increase in parliamentary seats, raising the maximum strength of the Lok Sabha to 850 and state assemblies to 650. Additionally, the seat allocations between states are proposed to remain frozen until 2051, retaining the 1971 Census as the distribution basis.

Reaction to the legislative push has prompted strategic discussions among opposition parties. INDIA bloc leaders convened at the residence of Congress president Mallikaljun Kharge to discuss a joint strategy, with some MPs raising questions about the proposed expansion. Meanwhile, Bahujan Samaj Party (BSP) chief Mayawati welcomed the renewed push for the 33 percent quota but demanded a separate sub-quota for women from Scheduled Castes (SC) and Scheduled Tribes (ST) to ensure equitable representation, while questioning the political intentions behind the concurrent delimitation exercise ahead of the upcoming Parliament session.
