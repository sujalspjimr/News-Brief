Severe Storms and King Tides Disrupt Thanksgiving Travel Across the United States

Severe storms bringing heavy rain, damaging winds up to 70mph, and major coastal flooding have disrupted Thanksgiving travel and caused widespread power outages across the United States. More than 80 million Americans were expected to travel for the holiday, but tracking site FlightAware reported that hundreds of flights were cancelled and thousands delayed on Thursday. The Federal Aviation Administration had warned of possible delays throughout the week due to the weather.

On the East Coast, states including Pennsylvania, Virginia, Maryland, and New Jersey experienced severe impacts, with nearly one million households reporting power outages by Thursday evening. Governors in New Jersey and New York declared states of emergency as communities faced flooding, closed bridges, and battered coastlines. In New York City, organizers postponed the annual Macy's Thanksgiving parade to Saturday. Washington DC recorded its wettest Thanksgiving on record, with tidal waters soaking runways at Reagan Washington National Airport.

Simultaneously, the West Coast faced severe weather as a series of Pacific storms brought rain, mountain snow, and strong winds. Record-breaking king tides peaked along California's coastline, with water levels reaching over eight feet in areas like San Diego and Los Angeles. The National Weather Service warned of potential coastal flooding, and Amtrak's Pacific Surfliner cancelled several trains due to track washouts.

Strobe Talbott, Former Journalist and Deputy Secretary of State, Dies at 80

Strobe Talbott, a prominent journalist who transitioned into diplomacy and later led a major policy think tank, has died at the age of 80. His son, Devin Talbott, confirmed that he passed away peacefully at his home in East Hampton, New York, following a long illness.

Talbott began his career as a journalist, spending 21 years at Time magazine where he focused on Soviet-American relations and served as Washington bureau chief and a foreign-affairs columnist. His early career included translating the leaked memoirs of Soviet leader Nikita Khrushchev while at Oxford, an assignment that secured his start at the magazine. Educated at Yale and Oxford—where he roomed with future president Bill Clinton—Talbott eventually joined the Clinton administration in 1994 as deputy secretary of state. In this diplomatic role, he shaped U.S. policy toward Russia and post-Soviet states and handled negotiations with India following its 1998 nuclear tests.

Following his government service, Talbott led the Brookings Institution as president from 2002 to 2017 and chaired the State Department's foreign affairs policy board. Over his lifetime, he authored more than a dozen books, with his final work published in 2020. He is survived by his wife, Barbara Lazear Ascher, two sons, a stepdaughter, siblings, and eight grandchildren.

Millions of UK Homeowners Face Rising Mortgage Payments

Millions of UK homeowners are facing significant increases in their monthly mortgage payments as they roll off fixed-rate deals over the coming years. According to new figures from the Bank of England's Financial Stability Report, about 6.5 million owners out of 7 million borrowers coming off fixed deals before December 2029 will see higher repayments. The Bank estimates an average monthly repayment jump of £100, which is more than double the £45-a-month hike estimated in its previous report. In the short term, roughly one million homeowners refinancing by the end of next year will experience an average increase of about £200 a month. Furthermore, about 430,000 homeowners face a jump of more than £500 a month.

The rising costs are driven by global economic uncertainty and higher interest rates. Following the start of the Iran war, average rates on two and five-year fixed mortgages have climbed to their highest levels in about three years, reaching roughly 6%. Earlier in November, the Bank of England raised its benchmark interest rate from 3.75% to 4%—its first hike in more than three years—to combat inflationary pressures, and financial markets have predicted further rate rises. Additionally, these mortgage pressures coincide with other cost-of-living challenges, including a 26% increase in the household energy bill cap announced by Great Britain's energy regulator.

While analysts have described the situation as brutal for households coming off low fixed deals agreed before 2022, officials emphasize that many households retain some financial resilience. Bank of England deputy governor Sarah Breeden noted that household debt is relatively low and most households possess savings buffers, making the aggregate shock absorbable. However, lower-income households and renters face greater vulnerability due to spending a larger proportion of their incomes on essentials, potentially forcing them to reduce discretionary spending or draw down savings.

UK Seasons Shifting With Fewer Frosts and More Hot Days, Met Office Reports

New analysis from the Met Office indicates that the UK's seasons are shifting, characterized by fewer winter frosts and an increase in hot summer days. According to the data comparing the decade up to 2025 with the period from 1961 to 1990, the number of winter days with an air frost has dropped by approximately a quarter nationwide, and by about a third in southern England. Conversely, the average number of UK summer days reaching 25C has more than doubled, while days reaching 30C have increased fourfold.

Met Office scientists attribute these seasonal changes to global climate warming, noting that summers are effectively widening while winters are shrinking. The shifting conditions have introduced notable regional variations. For example, Cornwall and the Isle of Wight have seen winter frost days almost halve, while Greater London experienced an increase in days reaching 30C to an average of 6.1 per summer. Provisional data also noted that the summer of 2026 was the hottest on record, featuring five widespread heatwaves.

These climatic shifts are having tangible impacts on agriculture, wildlife, and water resources. Met Office officials point out that extreme temperatures present physiological challenges for humans, animals, and crops alike. Farmers have faced water challenges ranging from planting difficulties caused by wet winters to insufficient water for crop growth during dry summers, with impacts described as seismic for horticulture.

UK Hospitals Brace for Severe Flu Spike Amid Surge in Admissions

Health officials in England are urging vulnerable populations to get vaccinated as soon as possible following a sharp increase in severe flu cases and hospital admissions. Data from the UK Health Security Agency (UKHSA) reveals that hospital admission rates soared by nearly 80% over a single week, reaching the highest levels for this time of year since before the Covid-19 pandemic. Driven largely by the H1N1 strain, flu levels are expected to rise further as indoor socialising increases ahead of the Christmas period. Outgoing NHS England chief executive Sir Jim Mackey warned of an upcoming "horrible spike" in cases, noting that more hospital beds are currently occupied by flu patients than at the same time last year.

In response to the growing winter pressures, some NHS trusts have already had to declare critical incidents, and certain hospitals have reintroduced mask-wearing for staff and visitors to curb the spread of respiratory illnesses. Officials have expressed concern over low vaccine uptake, noting that only about 68% of people aged 65 and over, and just 34% of the roughly 9.5 million individuals under 65 in clinical risk groups, have received their jab so far. The UKHSA emphasizes that individuals at increased risk of serious illness are significantly more likely to die if they contract the flu.

Alongside the immediate viral threat, health leaders raised alarms regarding systemic strains on the healthcare infrastructure, specifically condemning the normalisation of "corridor care" where patients are left on chairs and trolleys in corridors near A&E units. Sir Jim Mackey described the practice as unacceptable from both safety and personal dignity viewpoints. With the health service also dealing with previous pressures like summer heatwaves, experts warn that the early and aggressive onset of the flu season could severely strain services throughout the winter months.

Competition Watchdog Warns Private Dentistry Costs Are Too High for Millions

The UK's Competition and Markets Authority (CMA) has published an interim report warning that wide-ranging reforms are needed in the £8bn private dentistry market to ensure consumers get a fair deal. According to the watchdog, around four million people avoided going to the dentist over a two-year period because treatment was too expensive, with roughly 60% of UK patients now utilizing private care for some of their needs. The investigation flagged a catalogue of concerns, including large dental groups increasing prices by an average of about 7% annually between 2023 and 2025—outpacing both inflation and wage increases—and wide local price variations, such as fillings ranging from £80 to £223 in Preston.

The regulator also highlighted that patients face difficulties shopping around, noting that one in six practices fails to publish prices online. Furthermore, the CMA raised concerns that financial incentives and commercial targets could lead practices to push more expensive treatments, promote dental payment plans, or convert NHS patients to private care in ways that may not serve the patient's best interests. Underlying these market issues is a broader shortage of dentists, with England experiencing an estimated job vacancy rate of about 8%.

In response to these findings, the CMA has called for a shake-up of the sector and suggested that the government play a more direct market-shaping role. Meanwhile, the British Dental Association emphasized that while a thriving private market is essential, it is equally vital for the government to fix NHS dentistry.
