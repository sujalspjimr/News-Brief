COP31 Climate Talks Reach Deadlock Over Fossil Fuels and IPCC Timelines

Negotiations at the COP31 UN climate summit in Antalya, Turkey, have entered overtime and fallen into deadlock as nearly 200 countries struggle to reach a consensus on a final global climate deal. A major point of contention centers on a draft agreement that omitted references to reducing the world's demand for fossil fuels or transitioning away from oil, gas, and coal. This omission drew fierce pushback from a coalition including European Union nations, Panama, and island states, who argued that an agreement failing to address fossil fuels is unacceptable. Conversely, fossil fuel-producing countries and negotiators have defended focusing on implementation and clean energy targets.

Simultaneously, developing nations and regional groups like the Africa Group are pressing for more robust financial commitments to address adaptation and the impacts of climate change, with representatives asserting they did not come to the summit to beg. European officials, however, have suggested utilizing already pledged funds. Compounding the friction at the summit is an underlying dispute regarding the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) timeline. While the EU and various climate-vulnerable countries want the next IPCC assessment published by 2028 to inform the upcoming global stocktake, nations such as Russia, Saudi Arabia, India, and Iran have pushed for a delay until 2029.

With talks extending into the weekend, observers and campaigners have expressed deep concern over the direction of the summit. Activists have criticized the influence of fossil fuel interests and questioned the overall effectiveness of the COP process, while delegates continue working toward a revised final text, officially referred to as the Antalya Accords, which also acknowledges that the threshold for keeping global temperature rises below 1.5C has been missed.

Supreme Court Halts Relocation of Central Secretariat Library Books Amid Heritage Concerns

The Supreme Court has intervened in the planned relocation of the 135-year-old Central Secretariat Library, directing the Union government to halt the shifting of its extensive collection of books. The 135-year-old library, currently located in the Shastri Bhawan complex and housing over 8.5 lakh books—including rare volumes dating back to 1702—faced a directive to vacate by September 30 as part of the broader Central Vista redevelopment project. Taking suo motu cognisance, a Supreme Court bench criticized the initial relocation plans as an "ad hoc approach," emphasizing that cultural heritage extends to preserving historical books alongside physical infrastructure like the Lutyens neighbourhood.

In response to the court's scrutiny, the Centre submitted an affidavit detailing plans to permanently relocate the Central Secretariat Library and the Tulsi Sadan Library to the under-construction Kartavya Bhawan-4, which is scheduled for completion by December 2028. According to the government, approximately 5,583 square metres has been allocated for both libraries in the proposed building. Until construction finishes, the government proposed temporarily housing the books in the General Pool Office Accommodation complex in Netaji Nagar.

During court proceedings, amicus curiae Haripriya Padmanabhan raised concerns regarding the handling of library materials, describing the storage conditions at a facility used for the Tulsi Sadan Library as "atrocious." The bench also highlighted past instances where regional language books from the library remained boxed up for years following previous relocations. Solicitor General Tushar Mehta assured the court that the government values the literary collection as a cultural treasure and will work alongside the amicus curiae to resolve the issue while upholding its commitment not to move the books prematurely. The matter has since been referred to the Chief Justice of India for assignment to an appropriate bench.
