Yemeni Government Forces Retreat Near Red Sea as Houthis Advance

Saudi-backed Yemeni pro-government forces announced they were withdrawing from the Bab al-Mandab Strait along the Red Sea coast, leaving the strategically vital waterway under the control of the Iran-aligned Houthi movement. The Southern Armed Forces stated that the retreat from areas including Dhubab Airport was a necessary redeployment to secure the interim government capital of Aden—located roughly 60 kilometers away—and to address threats such as ongoing Houthi attacks and the siege of Taiz.

The shift marks a major setback for Yemen's internationally recognized government and its Saudi allies, ending a weeks-long offensive aimed at reclaiming ground lost during a rapid Houthi offensive in September. Analysts noted that the momentum in the country's decade-long civil war is tilting toward the Houthis, who have firmed up their grip on crucial port cities like Mocha and continued targeting Saudi territory and international shipping corridors.

The renewed escalation in Yemen has displaced hundreds of thousands of people and contributed to a surge in global oil prices. The United Nations and humanitarian groups have voiced grave concerns over civilian casualties, restricted medical and food supplies in besieged cities, and the broader humanitarian impact of the ongoing conflict.

Miguel Ángel Félix Gallardo, Infamous Mexican Drug Lord, Dies at 80

Miguel Ángel Félix Gallardo, widely recognized as a foundational figure in the modern Mexican drug trade and the head of the Guadalajara Cartel, has died at the age of 80. According to the security ministry of Jalisco state, he passed away from multiple organ failure at a private hospital in Guadalajara after being transferred from prison following a medical emergency.

Félix Gallardo earned notoriety during the 1980s cocaine boom, during which he established critical logistical partnerships between Colombian cartels—including Pablo Escobar's Medellín Cartel—and his own organization to smuggle drugs into the United States. Operating with extensive political and law enforcement influence, his cartel created a framework for modern drug trafficking that eventually fractured into organizations like the Sinaloa Cartel, famously involving figures such as Joaquín "El Chapo" Guzmán.

His criminal enterprise faced intense international scrutiny following the 1985 kidnapping, torture, and murder of undercover U.S. Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA) agent Enrique "Kiki" Camarena, whose death triggered a massive manhunt and severely strained U.S.-Mexican diplomatic relations. Arrested in 1989, Félix Gallardo spent decades behind bars and maintained his innocence regarding drug trafficking and the agent's murder, while his deteriorating health in later years led to unsuccessful legal efforts to secure house arrest.

Key facts

  • Miguel Ángel Félix Gallardo died at age 80 from multiple organ failure at a hospital in Guadalajara.
  • He was the co-founder of the Guadalajara Cartel and was often referred to as the "boss of bosses" or "the godfather."
  • He pioneered drug smuggling operations by partnering with Colombian cartels, including Pablo Escobar's Medellín Cartel, during the 1980s.
  • His arrest followed the 1985 torture and murder of DEA agent Enrique "Kiki" Camarena, which sparked a major U.S. manhunt.
  • He had been in state custody and imprisoned since his capture in 1989.

Proposed Youth Employment Strategy and National Insurance Changes Outlined

An independent review led by former Labour minister Alan Milburn has recommended sweeping changes to address youth unemployment and the UK's benefits bill. The report suggests gradually lowering National Insurance (NI) contributions paid by businesses for workers aged 21 to 24 over five years, beginning in sectors like retail and hospitality, to encourage companies to hire young people. The review also proposes a legal guarantee of job placements, training, or employment support, alongside removing under-25s' access to the health element of Universal Credit and personal independence payments (PIP), replacing them with standard support or a new allowance.

Work and Pensions Secretary Pat McFadden backed giving job support a legislative underpinning, noting that current assistance is vague. Prime Minister Andy Burnham described the response as a generational and societal shift, emphasizing early intervention and vocational training while asserting that benefits must serve as a backstop rather than a starting point for young people.

The proposals have prompted varied political responses. While government ministers support tying guaranteed job support with reciprocal responsibilities from youth claimants, opposition figures such as Conservative shadow work and pensions secretary Helen Whately argued that legally guaranteeing support makes little sense and that authorities should instead focus on cutting employer taxes directly. Additionally, potential cuts to existing benefit entitlements could face resistance from left-wing MPs.
