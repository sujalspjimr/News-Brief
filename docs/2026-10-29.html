At Least 68 Killed in High-Speed Train Collision in Central Spain

A high-speed train collision in central Spain has killed at least 68 people and injured dozens more, marking the nation's deadliest rail disaster in 13 years. The crash occurred shortly after 09:00 local time on a Wednesday morning roughly 140 kilometers (87 miles) south of Madrid, between the municipalities of Mora and Orgaz in Toledo province, along the line connecting Madrid to Seville.

The incident took place when a train operated by Spain's state-owned railway Renfe, carrying 397 passengers, struck the rear of a stationary train operated by private carrier Iryo, which had 147 passengers on board. The force of the impact derailed carriages, sent several falling down an embankment, caused at least one carriage to catch fire, and left passengers trapped among debris.

Spain's Transport Minister Óscar Puente stated that the Iryo train had halted due to a computer failure. Under emergency protocols, a 24-hour control center in Madrid assumed control of rail movements, but officials described an "inexplicable" decision by the center to direct trains to resume movement without imposing speed restrictions, resulting in human error following the initial technical malfunction.

The collision is the second major high-speed rail disaster to occur in Spain this year involving the same operators. In response to the tragedy, Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez pledged a comprehensive investigation into the incident, and Spain declared three days of national mourning as authorities confront mounting concerns over the safety and management of the country's rail infrastructure.

Key facts

  • At least 68 people were killed and dozens were injured in a rear-end collision between two high-speed trains in Toledo province.
  • A moving Renfe train carrying 397 passengers struck a stationary Iryo train carrying 147 passengers on the Madrid–Seville route.
  • The stationary train had stopped due to a computer failure before a central control office instructed trains to proceed without speed restrictions.
  • Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez announced a full investigation into the crash, and three days of national mourning were declared.
  • The disaster is Spain's deadliest rail crash in 13 years and its second major fatal train accident of the year.

Netanyahu Bloc Leads in Tight Israeli Election Without Clear Majority

Partial results from Israel's general election indicate that incumbent Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's right-wing bloc is leading its main rival, led by former military chief Gadi Eisenkot, but both remain short of a parliamentary majority. With roughly 85% to 90% of ballots counted, Netanyahu's Likud party along with far-right and ultra-Orthodox allies were on track for around 53 seats, while Eisenkot's Yashar party and allied opposition parties stood at roughly 48 seats in the 120-seat Knesset. Voter turnout reached 77.7%, marking the highest participation rate in more than two decades.

Arab-led parties achieved a strong showing, projected to secure an unprecedented 19 seats following high turnout from Israel's Arab population. However, almost all Jewish parties have previously pledged not to work with them, leaving the path to a governing coalition uncertain. With hundreds of thousands of absentee ballots still to be counted—including those from soldiers, diplomats, and hospital patients—final results are expected to take several days.

Once the results are officially confirmed, President Isaac Herzog will begin consultations with all political factions to determine which leader has the best chance of forming a governing coalition. Political analysts suggest potential paths to resolving the deadlock include a national unity government, a coalition involving Arab parties, or potentially triggering new elections if compromise proves impossible.

Judge Rejects Justice Department Bid to Stop Processing California Ballots

A federal judge has declined a request from the U.S. Justice Department to order California election officials to set aside and halt the processing of approximately 20,000 ballots belonging to individuals the Trump administration claimed were non-citizens. U.S. District Judge David Carter rejected the department's demand, writing in his ruling that the executive branch's late-stage intervention risked disenfranchising thousands of eligible voters and violated principles of state sovereignty.

The Justice Department had previously provided election officials in Los Angeles and Sacramento with a list of names, demanding a review within 48 hours and threatening legal action if the ballots were not sequestered. Local officials and reviewers raised concerns that the federal government's data contained inaccuracies and flagged individuals not actually on the voting rolls. Judge Carter noted that granting the request could potentially violate a 1993 federal law requiring states to complete systematic voter list maintenance no later than 90 days prior to an election.

Following the ruling, the federal government filed a notice of appeal, signaling that the legal dispute will continue. The Justice Department has also threatened to prosecute election officials who tabulate votes from non-citizens, while critics and legal experts argue the administration's legal actions lack adequate foundation and appear aimed at undermining confidence in election results.

Chancellor John Healey Delivers UK Budget Raising Taxes on Banks and Landlords

Chancellor John Healey delivered his maiden budget, introducing a package aimed at addressing the cost of living crisis driven by global economic pressures and conflicts in the Middle East. Central to the announcement is a £2.6bn household energy payment, providing a one-off £100 grant in January to approximately 20 million households in council tax bands A through D. To fund these measures and maintain fiscal rules, the chancellor doubled the additional profit levy on banks to 6%, increased tax rates on property and savings income by 2 percentage points, and raised levies on gambling and vape shops.

The budget also outlines longer-term structural changes to household energy costs, including plans to eliminate unfair daily standing charges on gas bills for 23 million homes by 2027 or shortly thereafter. Additionally, the threshold for business rates relief for retail, hospitality, and leisure businesses was increased from £12,000 to £18,000, while previously planned fuel tax increases were cancelled and electricity VAT cuts extended.

Reactions to the budget were mixed. Supporters viewed the measures as a steady, reassuring response that respects fiscal rules while offering crucial breathing space to households facing rising energy bills. However, critics and think tanks raised concerns that the support is poorly targeted, pointing out that the £100 payout misses the most vulnerable consumers. Furthermore, the OBR and housing analysts warned that higher taxes on property income risk deterring landlords, potentially reducing rental supply and driving up rents for tenants.

Federal Reserve Holds Interest Rates Steady Ahead of Midterm Elections

The US Federal Reserve voted to leave interest rates unchanged at its board meeting, maintaining rates at a range of 3.75% to 4% in its final decision before the upcoming midterm elections. Two of the 12 board members dissented from the decision, preferring a quarter-point rate increase. The pause follows a rate hike last month prompted by rising inflation, which has remained above the central bank's 2% target and annualized above 3% for most of the year.

During a press conference, Fed Chair Kevin Warsh stated that politics played no role in the deliberations and that upcoming elections were not discussed. Warsh emphasized that the central bank remains focused on price stability and is adopting a wait-and-see approach to evaluate upcoming economic data. While September's rate hike drew criticism from Donald Trump—who argued that rates should be 1% or less—Warsh declined to comment on political remarks regarding the board's composition, though he characterized the meeting as a "heck of a family fight."

Analysts noted that the timing of the election made radical action less likely, as the economy would have had insufficient time to adjust before voters go to the polls. Although most economists anticipate at least one more rate hike before the end of the year, central bank officials have indicated that increases do not need to occur at consecutive meetings. Meanwhile, the Federal Reserve announced plans to reduce its rate-setting meetings to seven times in 2027 and six times in 2028, down from the traditional eight annual meetings.
