Justice Department Brings Third Set of Federal Charges Against Former FBI Director James Comey

The United States Justice Department has filed criminal charges against former FBI Director James Comey for a third time, accusing him of leaking national defense information stemming from an October 2016 news article. The three-page filing in Washington, D.C., presented two counts through a criminal information rather than a grand jury indictment.

Comey and his legal team strongly denied the allegations, calling the prosecution an abuse of the Department of Justice. His lawyer, Patrick Fitzgerald, noted that the matter had been previously investigated twice under the first Trump administration with no charges brought. Legal experts and Comey's defense team have argued that the new charges should be dismissed because they may exceed the 10-year statute of limitations and because the filing bypassed standard grand jury procedures.

These charges mark the latest development in a history of tension between Comey and President Donald Trump, who fired Comey in 2017 during the investigation into Russian meddling in the 2016 election. Previous attempts by the Trump administration to prosecute Comey include an indictment last year that was later dismissed, and charges earlier this year regarding a social media post.

  • Comey's defense team filed a motion to dismiss, arguing the statute of limitations has expired and that the matter was previously investigated twice without resulting in charges.

Federal Appeals Court Denies Trump Administration Bid to Halt California Ballot Counting

A federal appeals court has ruled 2-1 against the Trump administration's request to block California from counting 20,000 mail-in ballots that the Department of Justice argued belonged to non-citizens. The administration had demanded that state election officials segregate the ballots until individuals on the list could verify their citizenship, leading to a lawsuit after state officials refused to comply.

The decision by the San Francisco-based Ninth U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals represents a legal setback for the administration's efforts to exert federal oversight over state elections ahead of the November 3 midterms. Critics, including voting rights groups and Democrats, have argued that the push is an attempt to sow uncertainty regarding election outcomes. Meanwhile, questions have been raised regarding the accuracy of the Justice Department's list, with Los Angeles election officials analyzing a small sample and finding that a high percentage were born in the United States.

The dispute is part of a broader conflict between California and the federal government over voting practices and alleged non-citizen registration. State officials, including California Secretary of State Shirley Weber and Attorney General Rob Bonta, have criticized the Justice Department's claims as vague and unsubstantiated, praising the court's ruling as a victory for voting rights. Conversely, the administration has maintained its focus on ballot integrity, with Donald Trump advocating for vigilance against voter fraud.

  • The Justice Department claimed the ballots belonged to non-citizens and demanded they be segregated until citizenship could be verified.

Judge rules Trump administration illegally fired former FBI director's daughter

A federal judge has ruled that the United States Justice Department acted illegally when it fired Maurene Comey, a federal prosecutor in New York and the daughter of former FBI director James Comey. Maurene Comey, who lost her position in July 2025, filed a lawsuit arguing that her dismissal was unlawful and driven by her father's criticism of President Donald Trump as well as her perceived political affiliations. In Monday's ruling, Judge Jesse Furman found that the termination violated the US Constitution and her right to a fair legal process, noting that while the attorney general has dismissal powers over federal prosecutors, they may only be exercised in specific manners and for permissible reasons.

Judge Furman ordered the administration to reinstate her and provide back pay covering the period from her termination in July 2025 until December 2025, when she accepted a new job. During her career as a federal prosecutor in Manhattan spanning roughly a decade, Comey handled high-profile cases involving Jeffrey Epstein, Ghislaine Maxwell, and Sean "Diddy" Combs, consistently receiving high accolades from colleagues and supervisors. Her legal representation hailed the decision as a victory for civil servants and the rule of law.

In response to the ruling, a Justice Department spokesperson stated that the agency disagrees with the decision and intends to appeal. Court filings from the department indicated that the firing decision was made by former Attorney General Pam Bondi. The case occurs against a broader backdrop of ongoing legal disputes involving the Trump administration and members of the Comey family, adding to existing public discussions regarding political motivations within federal law enforcement agencies.

The company ultimately aims to launch 50,000 satellites equipped with giant mirrors over the next decade to illuminate parts of the Earth during nighttime hours. The Federal Communications Commission recently approved the company's proposal to launch an initial demonstration satellite next year.

Other proposed applications include providing solar power for military operations, replacing broken streetlights, and bathing crops in sunlight via an app.
